Kevin Lee

University of California at Los Angeles, Los Angeles, CA NASA Jet Propulsion Laboratory, California Institute of Technology, Pasadena, CA kevinlee69720@g.ucla.edu

## Abstract

Cassini synthetic aperture radar (SAR) images reveal the dunes, plains, and lake basins of Titan, providing an instance of representations learned from Earth imagery for planetary terrain classification. Crossdomain Evaluation of Earth-to-Titan Transfer Using SAR (CETUS) compares features from DINOv2, DOFA and CROMA with classical image measurements and features from an untrained vision transformer on the U.S. Geological Survey’s Cassini SAR mosaic. The classifiers learn terrain labels from an expert geomorphological map and predict those labels in geographically separate Titan regions. Under logistic regression settings, pretrained encoders achieve higher mean macro F1 than the combined classical features. Encoder rankings change when feature scaling, optimization, and regularization change together. Further training on Titan improves DINOv2 performance, degrades DOFA performance, and leads to mixed results for CROMA under the tested settings. Architectural and input processing diferences prevent these comparisons from isolating the efect of pretraining. Classifier fitting and performance on individual terrain classes matter when assessing representation transfer for planetary mapping. Since the map draws partly on the same radar observations, the scores measure agreement with expert interpretation.

## 1 Introduction

Cassini synthetic aperture radar (SAR) images reveal Titan’s plains, dune fields, uplands, labyrinth terrain, lake basins and impact structures [1, 2]. Scientists combine their shapes and radar brightness with complementary observations to reconstruct the moon’s surface history. Image classifiers could help search this extensive collection. CETUS<sup>1</sup> tests this possibility with classifiers that learn broad terrain labels from an expert map in one set of regions and predict those map classes from SAR images elsewhere.

Planetary image classification already supports image search. For Mars, Wagstaf et al. [3] evaluated classifiers and deployed them for this purpose. Titan presents a related task, with uneven radar coverage and terrain units that difer greatly in extent. Accurate predictions for widespread plains can coexist with frequent errors on smaller units. A class label for a large image area also provides less detail than a geologist’s account of an individual landform’s shape, position and origin.

Pretraining on terrestrial imagery may provide useful visual features despite diferences in Titan’s surface, observing geometry and image processing. How well a classifier uses those features afects their evaluation. Poor fitting can obscure information in a representation, while an overall mean score can conceal opposing changes among terrain classes. CETUS examines classifier fitting and performance for individual classes using the same Titan mosaic and geomorphological map.

CETUS evaluates DINOv2, a general visual encoder pretrained on natural images; DOFA, which learns across Earth observation sensors; and CROMA, whose pretraining pairs terrestrial radar and optical imagery [4, 5, 6].

The input tiles sample the U.S. Geological Survey (USGS) Cassini SAR and HiSAR display mosaic [7]. Each image tile contains 128 × 128 pixels over a nominal 45 km by 45 km area in a local metric projection. The mosaic’s digital numbers, or DN, result from an 8 bit logarithmic stretch of calibrated source images. The tiles contain resampled values from that mosaic. Tiles are retained when finite positive SAR values occupy at least 80% of their pixels. These DN describe image brightness; obtaining physical radar backscatter would require radiometric information beyond this stretch. Pixel spacing determines sampling of the mosaic, while efective resolution and observing geometry vary among its constituent images.

The map of Lopes et al. [2] supplies six terrain labels: Plains, Dunes, Hummocky, Labyrinths, Lakes and Craters. The benchmark uses Hummocky for the map’s Mountains layer and Lakes for its Basins layer. Hummocky includes mountains and degraded mountains; Lakes includes filled and empty lakes and seas; Craters includes rims, ejecta, central peaks and fills. A 64 × 64 grid samples the map over each tile, and the most frequent valid class becomes its label. Ties go to the lowest class index. Label assignment retains every tile with a mapped class, even when that class occupies less than half of the valid samples. Its fraction describes the mixture of map classes in the image area, separately from confidence in the expert interpretation. Map samples and SAR pixels use diferent validity masks and may cover diferent portions of a tile.

Label assignment uses the first edition of the digital map polygons [8]. At a scale of 1:20,000,000, the global map simplifies regional mapping by omitting units smaller than approximately 30 km<sup>2</sup> [2]. Other

Cassini observations inform the interpretation, especially outside SAR coverage. The sampling grid therefore represents terrain divisions whose location and detail depend on that map. The Dunes class includes linear dunes and sand sheets; the Lakes class can identify either liquid or an empty basin. A classifier learns the predominant mapped unit in an image area, subject to these limits of scale and interpretation.

Figure 1 shows the SAR images together with their modal map labels. The class fractions make the choice reproducible. Whether each example is geologically representative still requires expert assessment.

The expanded catalog contains 23,380 tiles. It preserves all original image arrays, adds 12,009 tiles and changes 15 labels on existing tiles. Independent inverse projection and direct lookup of map pixels agree with the new sampler over the complete grid of possible image centers. Figure 2 shows where the additional images lie and how class counts vary among regions. Since SAR also informed the expert map, agreement with its labels measures reproduction of that interpretation.

## 3 Experimental design

## 3.1 Geographic separation and image normalization

Titan is divided into five longitude sectors delimited by 0, 72, 144, 216, 288 and 360 degrees east. Sectors are numbered from 1 to 5 in increasing longitude. Fold f tests on sector f, uses the next sector cyclically for validation and trains on the remaining three. The Selk holdout contains 65 images within 5<sup>◦</sup> of 6<sup>◦</sup>N, 161<sup>◦</sup>E. A conservative minimum distance of 108.640 km between centers of images in diferent sets gives a nominal 45 km separation between their square image areas. This excludes another 69 tiles near Selk, validation tiles near the test set and training tiles near the original validation or test sets. The primary split uses the remaining tiles without replacements. Geographic separation reduces proximity between the sets; spatial dependence can persist beyond the imposed distance.

The five test sets contain 23,246 distinct tiles. Training sets contain 12,348 to 14,644 tiles and validation sets contain 3,453 to 5,420; all contain the six classes. In each fold, image normalization uses up to 200 training tiles and 512 finite positive pixels per tile, with random seed zero. DN are scaled between the 1st and 99th sample percentiles and clipped to [0, 1]. Only training pixels determine these limits. Every feature family uses the same initial DN scaling for a given fold.

To test sensitivity to training geography, the replacement experiment reinstates all eligible training tiles removed by the separation rule. For each class, it removes as many retained tiles as it reinstates. Both classifiers use the same five training sets in each fold. Each set changes 2.20% to 3.96% of the training images in a fold. Each replacement set retains the 200 tiles that determine image normalization, so the transform stays the same. Validation and test tiles, training class counts and Selk exclusions also stay the same. For each of four encoders, logistic regression recalculates feature standardization and fits a classifier on each replacement set. The stochastic gradient descent (SGD) classifier uses five training seeds per set. This gives 100 additional logistic regression fits and 500 SGD classifiers. Section 4.5 describes the tile counts and the resulting score diferences.

## 3.2 Image representations and classifiers

Each encoder is a vision transformer (ViT) with 768 output features. DINOv2 and the Untrained ViT use the classification token, the representation assigned to the whole image. DOFA averages the image patch representations, excluding that token, and applies a final normalization layer. DINOv2 ViT-B/14 repeats SAR into three channels and applies ImageNet channel normalization. DOFA ViT-B/16 uses one channel and numeric band identifier 13.78. The upstream Sentinel-1 example standardizes its channels and uses 5.405 [9], whereas Appendix D of Xiong et al. [10] assigns SAR the identifier 3.75. CETUS omits the example’s standardization. How these choices relate to the model’s pretraining inputs remains uncertain. CROMA ViT-B/8 duplicates SAR into two channels, averages patch representations and passes the mean through its learned feedforward network. Both channels contain the same polarization information. Bilinear resizing gives $2 2 4 \times 2 2 4$ inputs, except for CROMA’s $1 2 0 \times 1 2 0$

The Untrained ViT baseline uses one ViT-B/14 with a single input channel and random seed 42. Its encoder receives no training: the supervised classifier learns Titan terrain labels from the features produced by its random weights. All classifier fits reuse this encoder, so the five SGD repeats measure variation in classifier training for that one set of encoder weights. Its single-channel input stem and lack of ImageNet channe normalization difer from DINOv2’s input processing. The comparison therefore combines pretraining with input diferences and cannot isolate the contribution of pretraining.

DOFA’s final feature normalization retains its initial scale of one and bias of zero because the checkpoint lacks those parameters. The layer still normalizes each feature vector. The checkpoint also contains parameters for a separate normalization layer that the evaluated path does not use. These missing and unused normalization parameters motivate checking the original input convention before interpreting DOFA’s performance or trying another training configuration.

For the primary comparison, each feature is standardized by its training mean and population standard deviation (SD) before multinomial logistic regression (LR) is fitted. The classifier uses an intercept, inverse class frequency weights, L2 regularization, $C = 1$ , LBFGS, tolerance $1 0 ^ { - 4 }$ and at most 2,000 iterations. The weights give each class equal total weight in the loss. Each feature family has one fit per fold; validation data do not choose among fits. Classical features comprise 13 intensity statistics, a 32-bin histogram, 1,820 texture features and their 1,865-dimensional concatenation. The texture measurements use cooccurrence matrices, local binary patterns, Gabor filters, histograms of oriented gradients and wavelets. Histogram and texture calculations also rescale each tile between its own 1st and 99th percentiles. Classifier settings are the same across families; feature definitions and dimensions difer.

The combined classical features provide the primary baseline for assessing whether an encoder adds predictive value beyond conventional image measurements. A separate majority predictor assigns every image to the most numerous class in the training fold. It uses class frequency alone, while classifiers using classical features also use image information.

The second linear classifier uses unstandardized encoder features and SGD. Its cross-entropy loss has inverse class frequency weights. The classifier is trained for 100 epochs with learning rate 0.01, momentum 0.9, zero weight decay, cosine decay and batch size 256, then evaluated at the final epoch for five classifier seeds in each fold. The common logistic regression settings were chosen after inspecting these SGD results, but before examining the logistic regression or combined classical results. The comparison changes feature scaling, optimization and regularization together.

A nearest-neighbor classifier provides a further comparison. It takes an unweighted vote among the 20 closest training features by cosine similarity and assigns ties to the lowest class index.

## 3.3 Training part of the encoder on Titan

For each pretrained model, training updates the final two transformer layers and a new linear classifier for all 50 epochs, with five optimization seeds per fold. The 75 fits use AdamW, learning rates of $1 0 ^ { - 4 }$ for the encoder and $1 0 ^ { - 3 }$ for the classifier, weight decay 0.01, cosine decay, microbatches of eight and accumulation over four microbatches. The loss uses inverse class frequency weights, and training uses automatic mixed precision. Image normalization, model inputs and geographic divisions stay the same. Evaluation uses the earliest epoch attaining the highest validation macro recall among epochs with optimizer updates. The new classifier has an independent initialization from the earlier linear classifiers. Appendix A.2 gives the training counts and checks of the saved models.

## 3.4 Performance measures and regional averages

For each of the six classes, precision, recall and F1 are calculated, with undefined ratios set to zero. Macro scores are the arithmetic means of these class scores; in particular, F1 is calculated before averaging classes. For SGD and encoder training, five seeds are averaged in each fold, followed by the mean and sample SD of the five fold values. Diferences involving encoder training compare the same folds, using the same seed for the SGD comparison and one logistic regression fit per fold. For encoder training, validation macro recall determines the epoch; the test results include all three metrics. The SD measures variation among these regions. Overlapping training sets can induce dependence among cross-validation scores [11]. Spatial dependence among Titan images is a further concern when treating these folds as independent geographic repetitions.

The primary average gives each region equal influence for every class, even when class counts difer. Scores are also calculated after combining confusion counts from all test folds separately for each encoder-training seed, then averaging the resulting seed scores. Logistic regression contributes one matrix across all test tiles. This second calculation weights the available test population diferently. Both calculations concern the sampled mosaic, with uneven geographic coverage and potentially several images per landform. Every primary comparison uses regional averages. The alternative weighting, distance checks and map polygon analysis were designed after inspecting the main results and use the existing predictions. The experiment replacing training tiles was specified before its results were examined.

Bold numbers identify the highest displayed mean within the comparison named in each table caption. Ties at the displayed precision receive equal emphasis. Bold type does not establish statistical significance.

## 4.2 Classifier fitting changes the encoder ranking

With SGD, DINOv2 leads at 37.71% F1, followed by CROMA at 35.26% and DOFA at 29.04%. Logistic regression puts CROMA first and raises the Untrained ViT result from 7.97% to 27.28%, an increase of 19.3 percentage points. For the Untrained ViT, the SGD classifiers assign at least 95.77% of each test set to one class; logistic regression predicts all six classes in every fold. Both fits learn from Titan labels and use the same encoder features. The higher LR score shows that these features permit better predictions than the nearly constant SGD outputs. Scaling, optimization and regularization change together, leaving their individual contributions unresolved. All pretrained encoders still exceed this particular random initialization in every fold with logistic regression. This comparison with one encoder initialization does not establish the advantage over random encoders in general. Figure 4 shows the change for each region.

Both classifiers include an intercept. In exact arithmetic, an invertible afine rescaling of features leaves the set of possible linear classifications intact. Rescaling changes numerical conditioning and the meaning of an L2 penalty in the original feature coordinates. Fitting choices can therefore change performance even when the representation stays the same. Identifying the contribution of scaling would require varying it separately from optimization and regularization.

Thus classifier choice changes both the encoder ranking and the tradeof between precision and recall on these images.

## 4.3 How training on Titan changes performance

Training the last two layers raises DINOv2 to 41.16 ± 2.60% macro F1. This exceeds logistic regression by 4.35 ± 0.34 percentage points and SGD by 3.45 ± 1.10 points, with both improvements present in every fold. DOFA reaches 15.68 ± 3.35%. Its diferences from the two classifiers are $- 2 0 . 2 3 \pm 4 . 4 5$ and $- 1 3 . 3 6 \pm 4 . 1 7$ points, respectively, and both are negative in every fold. CROMA reaches $3 9 . 1 7 \pm 2 . 3 1 \%$ , improving on SGD by $3 . 9 1 \pm 3 . 0 3$ points in the mean and in every fold. Against logistic regression, its diference is $- 0 . 1 7 \pm 1 . 1 2$ points, with two positive and three negative fold diferences. Figure 5 shows both comparisons. CROMA’s macro recall nevertheless increases by $1 . 7 0 \pm 2 . 6 2$ points relative to logistic regression. Whether encoder training improves performance therefore depends on both the comparison classifier and the performance measure.

## 4.4 Which terrain classes improve?

Relative to logistic regression, DINOv2 improves Dunes, Hummocky and Labyrinths F1 in every fold, with little change in mean Plains F1. CROMA gains on some classes and loses on others. DOFA’s decline spans all six class means. Figure 6 shows the regional variation behind these averages. Explaining why the encoders respond diferently would require experiments that vary the training choices separately.

DINOv2’s largest mean F1 gains are 10.37 percentage points for Labyrinths, 6.33 for Dunes and 4.90 for Hummocky. CROMA gains 2.94 points for Lakes while losing 2.44 for Plains and 2.13 for Dunes. DOFA’s Hummocky F1 falls from 49.71% to 0.42%, and its recall falls from 61.82% to 0.24%. Hummocky contains 2,757 test tiles. DOFA’s deterioration therefore extends to a class represented by many test images.

CROMA’s small change in macro F1 also depends on how regions are weighted. The diference is −0.17 percentage points when each fold receives equal weight and +0.85 when confusion counts from all test folds are combined separately for each training seed. With the latter calculation, DINOv2 still improves and DOFA still declines. Table 5 compares the two calculations; the primary results continue to give every region equal weight.

## 4.4.1 Craters remains dificult to predict

Every feature family has mean Craters F1 below 4% with logistic regression. For pretrained encoders, it ranges from 1.61% to 1.94%, with precision from 1.14% to 1.37%. Better macro F1 can accompany worse prediction of this small class. For example, changing DOFA’s classifier from SGD to logistic regression raises macro F1 from 29.04% to 35.91%, while Craters recall falls from 40.85% to 4.08% and Craters precision from 1.99% to 1.33%. Assessing predictions of crater terrain therefore requires the class scores as well as the overall score.

After encoder training, mean Craters F1 is 3.27% for DINOv2, 0.00% for DOFA and 1.95% for CROMA. DINOv2 reaches 4.64% Craters precision and 19.54% recall. Thus, despite its higher macro F1, most of its Craters predictions disagree with the map. Table 6 gives precision, recall and F1 for every class and encoder.

DINOv2’s Craters F1 becomes 5.15% when confusion counts are combined across test folds for each seed. The finding of F1 below 4% refers specifically to equal regional weights. Combining counts across seeds or weighting fold F1 by test size would answer still diferent statistical questions. These low scores, together with the scale of the map labels, leave practical crater detection to be assessed separately.

## 4.6 Map mixture and geographic variation with SGD

SGD predictions from the geographic evaluation allow errors to be compared by map composition, image coverage and region. Repeating these analyses for logistic regression or encoder training would require separate calculations.

Mixed terrain: The mixture analysis groups test images by map class and fold, then compares images whose modal map fraction is below 0.75 with those whose fraction is at least 0.90. Both groups must contain at least 20 tiles. Twenty of 30 class and fold combinations qualify, all outside Craters. After averaging classifier errors for each tile and weighting these combinations equally, the more mixed images have 19.04, 11.01 and 15.92 percentage points more errors for DINOv2, DOFA and CROMA. The thresholds were set before this analysis, after inspecting the overall scores. Mixed terrain, image conditions and local context can all contribute to the diference. This association motivates reporting class fractions alongside prediction scores. Confidence in the map remains a separate question.

Region and training repeats: For the pretrained encoders, macro F1 varies more among geographic folds than among training seeds in a given fold. Fold SDs are 3.56, 2.94 and 4.05 percentage points for DINOv2, DOFA and CROMA; the mean seed SDs within folds are 0.225, 0.110 and 0.061 points. Training images and class counts also difer among folds, so these quantities describe two kinds of variation without separating their causes. The Untrained ViT behaves diferently: each SGD classifier assigns 95.77% to 100% of test tiles to a single class. Its F1 of 7.97% falls below the 13.27% mean obtained by always predicting Plains, the most numerous training class. This collapse occurs for this random encoder with the stated SGD settings; the logistic regression results show that a diferent fit can use its features more efectively.

Where the mixture comparison applies: The eligible groups contain 5,804 images with modal fractions below 0.75 and 13,206 with fractions at or above 0.90. Plains, Dunes and Hummocky enter in all five folds, Labyrinths in three and Lakes in two. More mixed images have higher error in 17 of 20 groups for DINOv2, 15 for DOFA and 17 for CROMA. Nearby images may share terrain or observing conditions, so these sign counts describe the sampled groups without providing independent repetitions of a causal test. Figure 9 shows the class assignments, and Figure 8 displays variation with geography, training repeats and mixture.

A separate comparison groups images by valid SAR fraction, below 0.95 or at least 0.99, with the same minimum number of tiles. Thirteen class and fold combinations qualify. Mean error diferences are +3.87, +2.17 and −1.09 percentage points for DINOv2, DOFA and CROMA. Lower valid SAR coverage accompanies higher error for two encoders and slightly lower error for the third. Both analyses retain the catalog’s minimum SAR validity of 0.80 and examine mixture and coverage separately. Direct image inspection and an analysis of their combined efects would be needed to distinguish the contributions of each.

Errors in neighboring images: The spatial analysis calculates correlations in SGD errors between tiles of the same map class and fold, using center distances greater than zero and at most 45 km. Raw correlations are positive in 21 of 24 eligible groups for DINOv2, 23 of 25 for DOFA and 24 of 25 for CROMA. This indicates spatial structure in test errors. The smallest Craters groups contain too few images, and constant error values make correlation undefined in some other groups. Dependence remains unassessed in the omitted groups. Appendix A.4 gives the distance intervals, weighting and minimum counts. Measuring whether proximity to training images inflates a score, and by how much, would require an additional experiment.

## 5 Classifiers trained on Earth and a comparison with Venus

The earlier experiments train classifiers on Earth site labels and test them against Titan map classes. They use the original Titan catalog and spatial groups without separation zones. The current experiments instead train classifiers with Titan labels and test their geographic generalization. Table 12 distinguishes the two datasets and designs. The comparisons below equalize training counts and class composition or vary image preprocessing. Diferences in label quality, geographic sampling and radar products still complicate the interpretation of transfer between Earth and Titan.

The original Earth split contains 352 training tiles and 46 validation tiles. Sampling both at 45 km by 45 km with the same output spacing leaves diferences in native resolution, point-spread functions, incidence angles and speckle. ## 5.3 Venus as an unlabeled distribution comparison

The Venus comparison uses encoder features from 2,945 tiles of the Magellan SAR FMAP mosaic [12], acquired at 2.385 GHz in S-band. These images lack terrain labels, so the comparison concerns feature distributions. The comparisons in Table 16 draw equal numbers of images from each domain, using 398 Earth, 2,945 Venus and 11,306 Titan tiles outside Selk as the available populations. The input description names the FMAP product from right-looking observations [13]. The raster header confirms one 8 bit band with 75 m spacing; look direction and global coverage remain unverified from that header. Product documentation describes a quantized radar cross section normalized by an angular scattering law. Feature extraction uses its DN encoding directly, without conversion back to physical backscatter.

The comparison uses squared maximum mean discrepancy, MMD<sup>2</sup> [14]. MMD measures the distance between kernel mean embeddings. With a characteristic kernel such as the Gaussian radial basis function (RBF), population MMD equals zero exactly when the distributions agree. Estimates from finite samples carry sampling uncertainty. Even agreement between feature distributions would leave the physical interpretation of the images unresolved.

The comparison uses the squared biased estimator with an RBF kernel on feature samples of equal size X and Y:

$$
{ \widehat { \mathrm { M M D } } } _ { \mathrm { b } } ^ { 2 } = { \frac { 1 } { n ^ { 2 } } } \sum _ { i , j = 1 } ^ { n } k ( x _ { i } , x _ { j } ) + { \frac { 1 } { n ^ { 2 } } } \sum _ { i , j = 1 } ^ { n } k ( y _ { i } , y _ { j } ) - { \frac { 2 } { n ^ { 2 } } } \sum _ { i , j = 1 } ^ { n } k ( x _ { i } , y _ { j } ) .\tag{1}
$$

Each table entry averages estimates from 100 repeated samples. Comparisons involving Earth use 398 images per domain; the Venus and Titan comparison uses 2,000 per domain. For a given encoder, all three comparisons use the same bandwidth. Equal sample sizes within each pair do not remove possible diferences in finite-sample bias between comparisons. The estimates describe diferences between feature distributions. Relating them to classification performance, radar frequency, surface composition, atmosphere, incidence angle and image processing requires separate experiments.

## 6 Discussion

## 6.1 Relation to previous work

Geospatial foundation models: Pretraining on Earth observation data has produced several genera image encoders. DOFA [5] generates patch embedding weights from band identifiers across sensing modalities. CROMA [6] learns from Sentinel-1 radar and Sentinel-2 optical images of the same locations, with losses for cross-modal agreement and image reconstruction. DINOv2 [4] learns from natural images through self-distillation and supplies a general vision comparison. Section 3 gives the inputs and encoder outputs used here. SatMAE [15] and Prithvi [16] extend masked autoencoders to temporal and multispectral satellite imagery. These models establish the terrestrial context for the present experiments, alongside a growing literature on planetary foundation models.

Planetary image analysis: Planetary machine learning spans terrain classification, object detection and segmentation. Burl et al. [17] trained classifiers to detect volcanoes in Magellan SAR images of Venus three decades ago. Studies of Cassini SAR include fuzzy cognitive maps for Titan surface analysis [18] and lake segmentation [19]. Cloud instance segmentation [20] examines Titan’s atmosphere through another imaging modality. For Mars, Wagstaf et al. [21] trained AlexNet with ImageNet pretraining for two tasks: classifying rover images and classifying cropped landmarks in orbital High Resolution Imaging Science Experiment (HiRISE) images. AI4Mars [22] initially collected approximately 326K human annotation submissions for ∼35K rover images, combining multiple annotations per image for terrain segmentation aimed at autonomous driving. Mars-Bench [23] evaluates foundation models on Mars science tasks, and MOMO [24] learns from several Mars orbital sensors.

On Earth, PANGAEA [25] evaluates geospatial foundation models for segmentation, change detection and regression. CrossEarth [26] and CrossEarth-SAR [27] study segmentation in terrestrial domains absent from training, with the latter concentrating on radar. CETUS examines geographic prediction of Titan map classes and considers the Earth and Venus experiments separately in Section 5.

Radar interpretation and terrestrial analogs: Studies of individual Titan landforms combine morphology with physical constraints. Paillou et al. [28] use terrestrial radar and topography to model dune backscatter and examine how those models might apply to Titan. Cartwright and Burr [29] compare terrestrial and Titan radar observations to test fan formation mechanisms. Such interpretations require observations beyond an image classification score. CETUS predicts broad map units from one display mosaic; its disagreements identify images that warrant closer interpretation. Determining a scattering mechanism or surface material requires additional physical evidence.

Evaluating transferred representations: Kornblith et al. [30] compare classifier training on existing image features with fine-tuning across image classification datasets. Kumar et al. [31] identify settings where full fine-tuning performs worse under distribution shift than a linear classifier trained on the original features. These findings motivate evaluating the original representations before training them further. CETUS trains only part of each encoder under one set of settings. Testing the explanation based on feature distortion proposed by Kumar and colleagues would require a separate analysis.

Untrained representations: Saxe et al. [32] showed that random weights in convolutional square-pooling networks could produce useful features for a supervised image classifier. Their experiments also distinguished the contributions of architecture and weight learning and examined variation across random initializations. Those results concern convolutional networks. They motivate evaluating random features as a baseline, while the behavior of CETUS’s Untrained ViT must be assessed from its own predictions.

## 6.2 Model comparisons

Encoder rankings depend on classifier fitting in the geographic evaluation. CROMA has the highest mean F1 with logistic regression, while DINOv2 leads with SGD and after training on Titan. CROMA’s logistic regression result is already similar to its mean F1 after encoder training. DINOv2 improves over both classifiers in every fold, whereas DOFA declines against both. An encoder’s ranking is therefore conditional on the classifier fitting settings, the encoder weights allowed to change and the evaluation images. Encoder training also changes optimization, regularization, batch sizes, numerical precision, training duration and the epoch used for testing. The results do not separate those contributions, and uncertainty about DOFA’s pretraining inputs further limits interpretation of its decline. The scores measure agreement with the map under the evaluated configurations. Geological accuracy and usefulness in practice require independent evaluation.

A direct test of pretraining would compare learned weights with multiple random encoder initializations while holding architecture, input processing and classifier fitting constant. Training a small convolutional neural network (CNN) on the current Titan folds would add a compact supervised comparison. Both experiments remain future work. The existing CNN results in Appendix B use the original dataset and geographic divisions, so they cannot supply that comparison on the current folds.

The MMD and t-SNE analyses describe feature distributions in the original dataset. MMD depends on the encoder, kernel and sampling scheme; t-SNE displays a projection of the sampled features. Classification on separate Titan test images is needed to assess predictive usefulness. CORAL adjusts Earth feature statistics using Titan training features, and its test recalls quantify the predictive efect. The smaller MMD after CORAL uses the fitting samples and a newly estimated bandwidth, leaving a reduction on independent data under a common kernel untested. These comparisons also leave the physical causes of domain diferences unresolved.

## 6.3 Terrain interpretation and data limitations

Terrain predictions follow the categories and mapping scale of the reference map. Better agreement for Labyrinths alone leaves open whether a model recognizes dissection. A Craters prediction can refer to a rim, ejecta or fill within a large image area; identifying distinct impact structures is a separate task. Disagreements can arise from mixed terrain, simplified map edges, information available to the mapping team outside the SAR image, or model error. The scores do not distinguish among these possible causes. Examining the images alongside acquisition metadata and an independent geological interpretation would help distinguish them.

Using the same mosaic makes the inputs comparable across methods. Within that mosaic, SAR and HiSAR modes provide diferent spatial information, efective resolution varies geographically, and image rendering afects brightness and texture. A follow-up analysis could compare errors for the same map classes across verified observing conditions, with the comparison specified before its results are examined. It could test whether acquisition contributes to the observed disagreements between image predictions and map labels.

Nearby SGD test errors often covary within a map class and geographic region. Appendix A.4 gives the counts and assumptions behind those correlations. Quantifying any score inflation or choosing an adequate separation distance requires further experiments.

The expanded catalog and geographic divisions both difer from the original evaluation, so diferences in scores combine changes in longitude handling, regional grouping and spatial separation. The five sectors sample the available display mosaic unevenly in both observing conditions and terrain classes. Class mixture, spatial dependence and the small number of regions limit generalization beyond those images. Independent expert annotations would help assess disagreement with the map. Agreement with those annotations and reproduction of the map’s labels remain distinct evaluations.

An expert label review should distinguish the SAR images available to the classifier from the complementary observations used by the mapping team. It should include mixed images, classes with few examples and multiple regions. Agreement among experts would measure consistency under the stated viewing conditions; it would not set a universal ceiling on a classifier’s agreement with one map. Assessing geological accuracy requires observations with an appropriate independent basis. Further model training would not supply that independent evidence.

The Earth experiments also need better source labels. Their inputs include incorrectly named locations, ofshore images with uncertain terrain assignments and overlapping training and validation areas. Equalizing numerical brightness ranges leaves the underlying radiometric diferences unresolved. The current Titan experiments keep the image product and class definitions common to all models, allowing comparison of geographic map prediction and sensitivity to classifier fitting. Separating radar physics, image rendering and representation learning as causes of errors requires the additional physical and experimental evidence described above.

## 7 Conclusion

In the evaluated configurations, terrestrial representations contain useful information for predicting the terrain classes in Titan’s geomorphological map. Classifier fitting afects how that information translates into predictions: the leading encoder changes when feature scaling, optimization and regularization change together. Training the last two DINOv2 layers improves regional macro F1 over both classifier baselines and improves Dunes, Hummocky and Labyrinths F1 over logistic regression in every fold. CROMA’s small overall change conceals opposing changes among classes, while DOFA declines under its evaluated input and training settings. Craters remains poorly predicted, and its test tiles are concentrated in a small number of map components. The CETUS predictions allow these comparisons to be reproduced and identify disagreements for geological inspection. Independent evaluation is still needed before using the models for mapping or image search.

## A.4 How errors vary with distance

The spatial analysis groups the original SGD and nearest-neighbor test predictions into 30 combinations of fold and map class. At each tile, the SGD error is the mean of five binary classifier errors. In each group, unordered pairs of tile centers are enumerated using great-circle distance on a sphere of radius 2,574.7 km. Distance bins are (0, 45], (45, 90], (90, 180], (180, 360] and (360, 720] km. Symmetric Pearson correlation is calculated by entering each pair in both orders and correlating the two error values. Pairs receive equal weight, so a tile contributes once for each pair in which it occurs. A coeficient requires at least 20 tiles in the group, 20 participating tiles and variance above 10<sup>−12</sup> among the values being correlated. Otherwise the result includes the reason that the coeficient is undefined. This analysis uses the original classifiers; the primary logistic regression fits are evaluated separately.

The analysis also regresses the SGD error fraction or the nearest-neighbor binary error on coordinates on the unit sphere, the fraction of the tile occupied by its modal map class, and the valid SAR fraction, with an intercept and a separate fit for each group. The same correlations are then calculated from the residuals. Removing a fitted trend can create apparent spatial structure. For CROMA’s nearest-neighbor classifier in fold 4, all 76 Lakes tiles participating in the first distance bin are misclassified, giving zero variance before regression. Only one of the 102 tiles in that group is classified correctly. After regression, the residua correlation is 0.99693 across 52 pairs. The high coeficient thus characterizes the residual errors and ofers no evidence for a physical mechanism. CETUS’s reproduction documentation identifies the complete unadjusted and residual coeficients, sample counts, undefined cases and independent numerical reconstruction.

These correlations describe whether nearby test tiles tend to be misclassified together. Estimating leakage between training and testing, inflation of accuracy, or efective sample size would require further analysis. Section 4.5 gives the separate experiment, specified in advance, that changes which nearby tiles enter training.

## B.1.1 Choosing the image examples

The examples retain the original image choices. The statistics used to rank them were calculated from finite, strictly positive pixel values in the stored arrays before conversion for display. For the Earth display, missing-pixel fills are now masked using the catalog validity counts and excluded from the percentile stretch; the original image arrays and selection statistics are unchanged. For Earth and Titan, the score favors larger standard deviation and penalizes variation among row means. Plains examples receive an additional preference for standard deviations near the median among eligible images. For Venus, a contrast filter first removes unsuitable examples; the remaining image with the highest score, including the same row-variation penalty, supplies the first example. Each statistic is scaled linearly to the unit interval over eligible images. Five more images are then chosen successively, maximizing the minimum distance to those already chosen in mean, standard deviation and skewness.

## B.1.2 Cassini SAR imagery

The Titan images in Figure 10 use the USGS HiSAR mosaic through T104 described in Section 2 [1, 7]. Its 8 bit logarithmic display values retain image appearance but do not provide calibrated absolute $\sigma ^ { 0 } .$ . Physical comparisons of radar return would require radiometric products such as the floating point backscatter images in Cassini Basic Image Data Records (BIDR) [33].

Image area and valid pixels: Each Titan and Venus tile is sampled in a local azimuthal equidistant projection whose origin is the tile center. Earth tiles are windows in the local UTM output of SNAP processing. Every tile represents 45 km by 45 km with 128 × 128 pixels and a spacing of 351.5625 m/pixel. Local metric sampling avoids the change in physical width with latitude that would arise from equal pixel windows in the global equirectangular raster. At least 80% of output pixels must satisfy the validity rule for the corresponding image product. The original catalogs contain 11,371 labeled Titan tiles: 11,306 for cross-validation and 65 reserved near Selk. They also contain 398 labeled Earth tiles from 13 analog sites and 2,945 unlabeled Venus tiles. Titan counts range from 7,458 Plains tiles to 25 Craters tiles. Macro recall gives each class equal weight, making the very small Craters sample particularly consequential for interpretation.

The USGS mosaic ends at T104, earlier than the observations used to calculate the mission totals of approximately 46% surface coverage by full-resolution SAR and 74% by SAR with HiSAR [33]. This evaluation covers only the image areas retained from the T104 mosaic.

Image normalization: For each Titan training fold, the 1st and 99th percentiles of a limited sample of display DN define the linear normalization. The Titan transformation uses the display values directly, without an additional logarithm or dB floor. Its output remains display data; recovering calibrated $\sigma ^ { 0 }$ would require a diferent input product. For Earth, calibrated Sentinel-1 $\sigma ^ { 0 }$ is converted to dB and scaled using training statistics. Venus FMAP values are normalized in their encoded DN space. Each transformation prepares model inputs in the units available for that product, retaining the diferences in their radiometric meaning.

## B.1.3 Map labels

Both Titan evaluations use the map classes and modal voting rule described in Section 2. Using the same classes permits comparison of the two evaluations against the map. Extending those labels to Earth also requires evidence about the surface materials and class distributions represented at terrestrial sites.

Recovering omitted western longitudes: The original sampler interpreted the map raster’s 0 to 360 degree longitude convention incorrectly. Reprojection returned missing data for valid western labels, and catalog generation then discarded the corresponding tiles. Explicit longitude wrapping and a smaller warp approximation tolerance recover 12,009 tiles, including 115 Craters. The new catalog preserves all 11,371 original image arrays and centers while changing 15 of their modal labels and 1,727 class fractions. Independent inverse projections and direct indexing of the map raster agree with all 168,501,248 samples at the 41,138 possible tile centers. The resulting catalog contains 23,380 tiles with at least 80% valid SAR coverage and mapped terrain, including 140 Craters. These checks verify sampling of the source map. Geological accuracy and classification performance require separate evidence. The main text evaluates the enlarged catalog using geographic sectors with separation zones; CETUS’s reproduction documentation links the label and catalog checks.

Independent repeat annotations and a numerical estimate of map error remain unavailable. A classifier can reproduce an error in the expert map, so agreement with that map may exceed agreement with independently established geology.

## B.1.4 VIMS and ISS display metadata

The catalog also contains display values from the combined VIMS and ISS mosaic of Seignovert et al. [34]; processing of its VIMS component is described by Le Mouélic et al. [35]. ISS is the Imaging Science Subsystem. The original sampler supplied longitude and latitude in degrees to a raster transform expressed in metres on a Titan sphere of radius 2,575,000 m. It also used coordinates of pixel corners as array indices. Consequently, all 23,380 auxiliary triplets in the enlarged catalog come from just six pixels near the map origin. A separate CETUS file uses the correct coordinate units and pixel centers and passes independent projection and interpolation checks. The previously distributed catalogs retain their original values. These channels describe a rendered composite. Quantitative spectra, radiances and band ratios would require calibrated VIMS products, and describing an entire image area would require an explicit rule for combining measurements across that area. CETUS restricts these triplets to display metadata. Terrain classification, comparisons of feature distributions and Selk scores exclude them. ## B.1.5 Earth source sites

The Earth sites were chosen as morphological analogs for Plains, Dunes, Labyrinths, Lakes and Craters. The Earth set contains no Hummocky examples, so transfer metrics omit that class. The site labels describe intended landform analogies. Equivalence in material, radiometry or formation process remains unestablished. Several assignments need review at the scale of individual images. Sixteen training tiles labeled Labyrinths come from an Egyptian region incorrectly named Wadi Rum in the catalog. The Sentinel-1 image extents establish the location error; changing the name alone would leave the terrain assignment unverified. The full-resolution GSHHG shoreline map shows no mapped land in 42 of the 77 Arctic tundra training areas, and marine regions occupy more than half of 65 such areas. One Icelandic Plains tile is mostly ocean in both global and national shoreline maps. These fractions use the nominal image areas without the SAR validity mask. The shoreline maps also leave sea ice and open water indistinguishable at the acquisition dates. A review of the actual image areas is therefore needed before using their uniform site labels as terrestrial Plains examples. The internal geometry analysis is identified in CETUS’s reproduction documentation.<sup>2</sup> Appendix C lists the sites and the intended morphological rationale. Earlier SAR studies illustrate how terrestrial landforms can inform comparative geomorphological interpretation [28, 29, 36].

ESA SNAP processing of Sentinel-1 GRD applies a precise orbit file, removes thermal noise, calibrates VV to linear $\sigma ^ { 0 }$ , and applies Range-Doppler terrain correction with the Copernicus 30 m DEM. The terrain-corrected output is then sampled at 351.5625 m/pixel over each 45 km image area. GRD processing already includes detection and averaging of radar looks, called multilooking. The SNAP sequence adds no further averaging before coarse resampling. The source spacing of about 10 m describes the sampling grid, rather than the efective spatial resolution. Pixels that are invalid or marked as layover or shadow by SNAP enter the encoder at the −40 dB floor. They are excluded from each tile’s dB statistics and valid-pixel fraction. For every retained tile, the median local incidence angle and the fraction marked by that mask are also retained.

Earth validation uses whole Sentinel-1 products as groups. Slices from one acquisition and repeat views of the same terrain can nevertheless enter both training and validation. Reconstructing the nominal image areas shows that 21 of 46 validation tiles intersect training areas; two Mistastin pairs overlap by about 99.97%. Grouping by product therefore permits geographic overlap between training and validation. For transfer, a reproducible rule reserves one product per Earth class for validation. The 46 validation tiles include the only product from one site, leaving 352 training tiles across 12 sites. The resulting classifier is evaluated on the five Titan classes represented in Earth training.

## B.1.6 Geographic folds in the original evaluation

The original Titan evaluation uses five-fold spatial block cross-validation. Here the blocks are metric regions approximately 250 km across, with longitude divisions adjusted for latitude. A reproducible greedy assignment keeps each region together while balancing class counts and total image counts across folds. Each run tests one fold, uses the next for validation and trains on the remaining folds. Neighboring regions may have diferent roles, with no additional separation zone between them. Nearby images can therefore remain correlated between training, validation and testing. Training sets also overlap across runs, so independence of the five test-fold estimates has not been established. The 17 Craters test tiles receive their labels from four polygon parts in the source map. Parts 14 and 18 contribute both training and test tiles in individual runs. These indices describe map geometry and cannot be equated with named or independent physical craters. They establish that training and testing include portions of the same mapped terrain, with an unmeasured efect on performance. Using the benchmark’s spherical Titan radius of 2574.7 km, 1,963 of the 11,306 distinct test centers lie less than 45 km from a training center in the corresponding run; only 79 lie less than 44.9 km away. Many distances are close to the nominal 45 km grid spacing, making counts sensitive to the radius and strict cutof. These measurements describe proximity of centers. Image-area overlap, similarity of radar texture and any inflation of performance require separate measurements.

The Selk region is removed before assigning the other regions to folds and is excluded from every training, validation and test set. Results are summarized over the five test folds. Repeated SGD training runs describe variation from initialization and minibatch order within each fold; they do not create additional independently sampled regions.

## B.2 Encoders and classifiers for the original dataset

## B.2.1 Encoder configurations

The four encoders use ViT-B [37] and return 768-dimensional feature vectors. The comparison evaluates complete configurations: pretraining, patch sizes, input transformations, conditioning and architecture details vary together.

DINOv2 and CROMA use the image dimensions, channel duplication and feature averaging specified in Section 3. DINOv2 was pretrained on 142M natural images [4]. CROMA averages its patch encodings and passes that mean through the learned feedforward transformation for SAR [6].

DOFA ViT-B/16: A hypernetwork generates patch embedding weights from a numeric band identifier [5]. Titan normalization and feature averaging follow Section 3. The original Earth features use a diferent band identifier from the repository example. The DOFA repository example supplies 5.405 for Sentinel-1; CETUS extends this frequency convention to Cassini Ku-band with 13.78. Optical bands use wavelength units. Inspection of the extraction records and arrays establishes that the original five-fold classification used 13.78 for both Earth and Titan. The earlier DOFA paper gives 3.75 for Sentinel-1, leaving the identifiers used in pretraining this checkpoint unresolved [9, 10]. Arrays generated separately for preprocessing comparisons and Venus need their own verification. Section 5.1 describes later experiments using Earth 5.405 and Titan 13.78, together with other changes to the experimental design. Measuring the efect of the identifier alone would require an otherwise identical comparison.

Untrained ViT in the original dataset: The original Untrained ViT configuration specifies a ViT-B/14, a single-channel patch stem and seed 42. Its encoder remains untrained while the classifier learns the labels supplied by the corresponding training domain. Feature standard deviations are approximately $1 0 ^ { - 4 }$ per dimension, and the classifiers perform near the class-frequency benchmarks. Because this result comes from one random state, it gives limited evidence about random features in general. Section B.4.4 separates the valid repeated MMD estimate from the numerical failure in the original permutation calculation. The code specifies seed 42, but the architecture chosen by its fallback branch and the exact initial weights remain unverified. Five classifier seeds therefore cannot be interpreted as five independently initialized encoders.

## B.2.2 Fitting classifiers and calculating metrics

With encoder weights unchanged, each model returns 768 features through the averaging or classification-token representation described in the main methods. All 168 tensors loaded from the original DOFA checkpoint were verified exactly. The normalization constants supplied when the model is constructed and the checkpoint normalization parameters that remain unused are the same as in the current evaluation; their separate efects on performance remain unknown. The linear classifier is fitted for 100 epochs with inverse-frequency class weights, and the final epoch is evaluated on the test images. The SGD implementation does not use the reserved validation fold for model selection. Its main metric is macro recall, the unweighted mean of recall over classes present in the evaluation set, equivalent here to balanced accuracy. Cohen’s κ compares agreement with that expected from the marginal prediction and label frequencies. Its dependence on class frequencies difers from that of macro recall, and per-class precision supplies additional information. Both metrics are calculated for five training seeds in each geographic fold. A cosine k-nearest-neighbor classifier supplies a comparison without fitting a linear decision rule. Appendix D lists the image inputs and weight sources.

For the Titan-only experiment, classifiers are fitted to Titan training data and predict the reserved Titan test fold. Additional comparisons on the original dataset include prediction of the majority class, random prediction using class frequencies, raw intensity moments, intensity histograms, gray-level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM), local binary pattern (LBP), Gabor, histogram of oriented gradients (HOG) and wavelet texture features. The original CNN results do not evaluate the current geographic folds. The metadata call this procedure transductive, but the transformation uses only training features. MMD calculated from the fitting features describes agreement on those training samples.

## B.3 Comparing feature distributions

## B.3.1 Estimator and sampling details

Section 5.3 defines MMD and the comparisons between Earth, Titan and Venus image collections. The primary estimate uses the median heuristic for kernel bandwidth and averages at least 100 reproducible subsamples with equal N from each distribution. Estimator sensitivity is examined with the unbiased U-statistic, a permutation test using one bandwidth throughout, omission of each Earth site in turn, and resampling of Titan geographic regions with replacement. Section B.4.5 gives the results, with complete values in CETUS. The site omissions cover the 12 sites in Earth training; validation contains the only product from the thirteenth site. Titan resampling uses the same geographic regions that define the folds. Dependence between neighboring regions remains unmeasured.

Proxy A-distance [38] provides a second measure of how readily a classifier distinguishes Earth from Titan features. The original calculation used diferent numbers of images from the two domains. The estimates for all twenty combinations of model and fold were subsequently recomputed from the verified original training features, with equal domain counts, a stratified half split, standardization fitted on the training half used for the domain classifier, hinge-loss SGD and balanced test error. Both calculations use the same data population but difer in sampling and classifier fitting. Table 23 reports the original estimates; the later estimates are given below. Both measure domain discrimination; assigning a physical cause requires other evidence. Distances between class centroids are also descriptive and use only classes labeled in both domains.

Attributing these diferences to radar scattering would require compatible calibrated products and measurements that account for incidence, polarization, spatial resolution, roughness, dielectric properties and preprocessing. HiSAR and FMAP supply display values here. The rendering experiments test model sensitivity to those display inputs. Recovering calibrated planetary backscatter would require suitable radiometric products.

## B.4 Further results on the original dataset

## B.4.1 Training and averaging across folds

Feature extraction uses batches of 64 images. Bilinear interpolation resizes each 128 × 128 tile to 224 × 224 pixels for DINOv2, DOFA and the Untrained ViT, or 120 × 120 pixels for CROMA. Linear classifiers train in batches of 256 for 100 epochs, with five training seeds per fold. Averaging estimates from the five training seeds gives one estimate per fold. The reported mean and sample standard deviation are then calculated across the five folds. The 95% Student-t intervals and paired tests supplied with the results assume independent fold estimates. Overlapping training data and possible correlation between neighboring test regions make that assumption uncertain, so these calculations are interpreted as exploratory.<sup>3</sup> Diferences use unrounded values and may difer by up to 0.1 from subtracting rounded table entries. The k-NN classifier uses k = 20 and gives the same result each time for a given fold. The distribution comparisons use 100 reproducible MMD subsamples of equal size per fold, the exploratory fold comparisons and the sensitivity calculations in Section B.3.1. All results here use the original population, including the omission of western Titan tiles.

## B.4.7 Selk uncertainty and disagreement

The Selk tiles are examined only after model development. They are excluded from every training, validation and test fold. For each tile, linear classifiers trained on Titan produce class probabilities and predictions from encoders whose weights remain unchanged. The ensemble comprises $N = 1 5$ combinations of pretrained model and geographic fold: three models across five folds. Entropy is calculated from the mean probability vector, $\begin{array} { r } { H ( \bar { \bf p } ) = - \sum _ { c = 1 } ^ { 6 } \bar { p } _ { c } \log \bar { p } _ { c } } \end{array}$ , and disagreement as $D = 1 - { n _ { \mathrm { m a x } } } / { N }$ , where $n _ { \mathrm { m a x } }$ is the number of members giving the most frequent class prediction. The Untrained ViT and the VIMS display values are excluded.

Entropy and disagreement are each scaled to [0, 1] using their own minimum and maximum among the 65 Selk tiles. The arithmetic mean of the two scaled quantities ranks the tiles within this set of models and images. A high score may arise from ambiguous map classes, transitions between units, image defects, model limitations or other unresolved causes. The ranking describes variation in classifier uncertainty and disagreement. Using it for anomaly detection, calibrated confidence or risk, geological prioritization or mission operations would require independent validation.

## B.4.8 Transforming features and training encoder layers

Two ways of using Titan information are compared: CORAL uses unlabeled Titan features to transform Earth features, while supervised training of the final transformer blocks uses Titan labels.

CORAL feature transformation: CORAL [41] aligns Earth feature statistics with those of Titan. The transformation subtracts the Earth mean, transforms the covariances, then adds the Titan mean: $\begin{array} { r } { F _ { \mathrm { a l i g n e d } } = ( F - \mu _ { \mathrm { s r c } } ) \cdot C _ { \mathrm { s r c } } ^ { - 1 / 2 } C _ { \mathrm { t g t } } ^ { 1 / 2 } + \mu _ { \mathrm { t g t } } } \end{array}$ . The transformation is applied to Earth features before fitting the linear classifier; encoder weights remain unchanged. The sample covariance matrices $C _ { \mathrm { s r c } }$ and $C _ { \mathrm { t g t } }$ each receive $1 0 ^ { - 5 } I$ . Eigenvalues are floored at $1 0 ^ { - 5 }$ before taking square roots and inverse square roots. Subtracting the source mean is part of the original formulation; adding the target mean specifies the coordinates used here. Table 26 compares classification before and after this transformation.

CORAL changes macro recall by −2.0 (p = 0.017), +1.0 (p = 0.370) and +2.9 $( p = 0 . 4 1 7 )$ percentage points for DINOv2, DOFA and CROMA, respectively. These exploratory paired tests use overlapping folds without adjustment for multiple comparisons. The small $\mathrm { M M D ^ { 2 } }$ after transformation comes from the same features used to estimate CORAL, with bandwidth estimated again. Measuring a reduction of discrepancy on independent data would require test features and one kernel used for both measurements. The remaining classification errors leave their class-dependent and physical causes undetermined. Aligning physical radar intensity would require compatible calibrated products and accounting for acquisition conditions.

Training the final two transformer blocks: The last two transformer blocks of each encoder are trained on Titan images. Table 27 averages five training seeds in each geographic fold. For comparison, linear classifiers are fitted again on features from encoders whose weights remain unchanged, using the same folds and seeds, so their values difer slightly from Table 19. These experiments used the implementation before weighted gradient accumulation was corrected. Table 4 reports results from the current implementation and enlarged catalog. Because the dataset and training design also changed, comparing these scores cannot isolate the efect of the correction.

CROMA loses 3.7 percentage points of macro recall while gaining 0.100 in κ, indicating a redistribution of errors among classes. DOFA declines by 27.2 percentage points and 0.171 in κ. These values apply to the original dataset and training code. The enlarged catalog and corrected implementation are evaluated separately. Attributing the older changes to architecture, conditioning, optimization or class imbalance would require comparisons that vary those factors independently.

## B.4.9 Recall and labeled training-set size

Figure 16 shows how macro recall changes as the number of labeled Titan training tiles increases, with encoder weights unchanged and a linear classifier fitted to their features. Stratified sampling guarantees at least one tile from each represented class. Small subsets therefore contain disproportionately many rare-class examples and require labels before images can be sampled. The curves apply to this sampling rule. Using them to forecast an annotation campaign would require a design that accounts for how labels are acquired. At 10 labels, CROMA reaches 26.8%, DINOv2 25.1% and DOFA 20.2%, compared with the 16.7% six-class benchmark. Using every training tile in each fold, DINOv2 reaches 50.3%, CROMA 44.4% and DOFA 34.9%. DINOv2 improves most consistently as labels are added, while CROMA is nonmonotonic at low counts. The loss here gives examples equal weight, whereas the experiment in Table 19 uses class weights. These diferent losses can yield diferent scores even when both experiments use every training tile.
