# Evaluation-Guided Demonstration Refinement for Vision-Language-Action Policies

The Winning Entry in the MetaFine Open Competition

Lifan Zheng<sup>1</sup> Guotao Chen<sup>1</sup> Zhenyang Li<sup>2</sup> Shujie Cui<sup>3</sup> Qiang Huang<sup>4</sup> Fan Liu<sup>1,∗</sup>

Technical report for RoboFineMani @ CoRL 2026

Abstract: We fine-tuned a separate $\pi _ { 0 . 5 }$ policy for each of the five tasks in the MetaFine Open Competition, with a task-specific training mixture and number of actions executed per prediction. Training started from the official demonstrations and expert procedures. Local evaluation exposed recurring grasping, pushing, and insertion failures. We addressed them by revising the demonstrated motions and by adding expert continuations from states the policy reached, while keeping nominal demonstrations in training. Checkpoints were compared on every instruction, so that a gain on one instruction could not hide a loss on another. Our submission, MAI, ranked first overall with a score of 96.66.

Keywords: Fine-grained manipulation, vision-language-action models, imitation learning

## 1 Introduction

MetaFine [1] evaluates fine-grained manipulation through instruction understanding, perceptual robustness, and behavior. The MetaFine Open Competition [2] covers five tabletop tasks: bottle-part grasping (T1), mug grasp-and-move (T2), switch toggling (T3), block sorting (T4), and letter insertion (T5). Understanding U measures success across instructions; perception P averages normalized areas under camera and lighting degradation curves; behavior B measures task success, or normalized stage completion for the multi-stage T2 and T4. Each task receives a composite score $S = ( P + U + B ) / 3$

We fine-tuned one $\pi _ { 0 . 5 } \ [ 3 ]$ policy for each task. We used MetaFine’s per-instruction scores, together with our own grouping of episodes by scene layout, to choose which trajectories to inspect, and revised the demonstrations where those trajectories showed recurring errors. The submitted policies scored 96.66 overall and ranked first on the final leaderboard.

## 2 Technical Approach

## 2.1 Policy and training setup

We fine-tuned all parameters of $\pi _ { 0 . 5 }$ using OpenPI [4] and the flow-matching behavior-cloning objective [5, 6]. The inputs are the instruction, seven arm and two finger joint positions, and RGB images from the base and wrist cameras. Each predicted action contains seven joint deltas and a gripper command. The policy generates a chunk of 50 actions using ten denoising steps, then executes the first 20 actions for T1, 30 for T3, or 10 for T2/T4/T5 before replanning.

We trained with AdamW [7], learning-rate warmup, and cosine decay. In the final training rounds, T2 started from the base model and each other task from its previous policy. Checkpoints were selected by local evaluation (Section 3).

## 2.2 Refining the official demonstrations

Training used the official demonstrations and trajectories generated by the official expert procedures. We ran these procedures on additional scenes and revised them in response to the failures found during development. Only successful demonstrations with consistent recorded observations and actions entered training. Where demonstrations contained unnecessary pauses or repeated approaches, as in T1 and T5, we shortened or replanned them. Variation in object pose and task geometry was kept, as were the motions needed to finish the task.

Some failed rollouts reached states that nominal demonstrations rarely cover. From such states the expert recorded continuations, following the rationale of dataset aggregation [8]. We also built recovery demonstrations from constructed failure configurations, such as a grasp on the wrong bottle part in T1, and excluded the actions used to set up these configurations from training. Expert procedures could use simulator state; deployed policies received only images, joint positions, and language. When continuations or recovery demonstrations were used, task-specific source quotas kept nominal demonstrations in every training batch.

T1: grasping the instructed bottle part. Local rollouts showed slips as lifting began. We adjusted the trajectories for both the cap and body instructions, shortening hesitation and beginning a gradual lift during closure. Nominal demonstrations, expert continuations, and recovery demonstrations remained in training. We evaluated the two instructions separately because earlier variants had improved body success at the expense of cap success.

T2: mug grasp-and-move. Batches balanced official demonstrations with trajectories generated by the official expert on additional scenes, with equal coverage of the three motion directions. We kept this recipe for the final submission after checking nominal and perturbed conditions.

T3: switch toggling. Local failures included missed contact and incomplete toggles at difficult approach angles. We revised the approach and extended the push through the toggle, with recovery demonstrations for failed contact. Later training added approaches that pass above the switch center, alongside the earlier demonstration sources.

T4: block sorting. In crowded layouts, the gripper sometimes closed without the target block. We adjusted the expert approach to leave clearance from neighboring blocks and recorded expert continuations from these empty-grasp states. Training mixed these trajectories with the official and earlier expert demonstrations.

T5: letter insertion. Earlier rounds replaced the T5 demonstrations with continuous planned trajectories that use one consistent grasp per letter. The remaining failures on o involved lateral offsets and release before seating. We revised the o trajectories to align the letter above the opening and seat it fully before release. The revised o demonstrations included trajectories with varied in-hand offsets, expert continuations from policy rollouts, and recoveries from a letter resting on the board. They replaced the earlier o demonstrations; C/R/L demonstrations were unchanged.

The earlier T3 revisions produced large gains, but the final revision did not establish a further improvement. Across two rounds, S on the 80-scene development suite rose from 74.9 to 91.6 and then 99.2. That policy is the reference in Table 3. We compared it with the selected policy on 120 scenes: the development suite, a 20-scene reserve set used to compare checkpoints, and 20 further scenes. With four inference seeds per model, the selected policy scored just 0.24 points higher (computed before rounding). The 95% scene-bootstrap interval, [−0.26, 0.79], includes zero, leaving the difference uncertain.

T4’s clearest gain was in crowded layouts: successes increased from 313/348 to 348/348 on 116 scenes, eliminating the 35 failures observed with the reference. Both policies already had nearceiling standard-suite S (Table 3). The selected policy also succeeded in all 720 nominal instruction episodes of the standard 80-scene suite and failed only once in 4,452 episodes on a separate 1,484- scene set. The larger-set result is reported without a reference comparison.

T5 improved chiefly on o, the letter targeted by the final revisions. Success on o rose from 82.5 to 97.5 on the development set and from 87.5 to 97.5 on the separate set; S also increased on both. The overall gains came with a small regression on C: development-set success fell from 100 to 98.75, despite its demonstrations being unchanged.

## 4 Lessons Learned and Discussion

Per-instruction scores and scene grouping revealed failures hidden by the task score. MetaFine’s separate instruction scores distinguished cap from body in T1 and isolated the letter o in T5. In T4 the task score was already high, and our own grouping of episodes by layout showed that the remaining failures concentrated near neighboring blocks.

Inspecting the few steps around contact helped distinguish failures that scores alone could not explain. In T1, T3, and T5, we observed a grasp that closed and then slipped, a push that stopped short, and a letter released above the slot, respectively. In response, we revised those segments of the demonstrations: a gradual lift during closure for T1, a longer push for T3, and full seating before release for T5.

Targeted revisions could introduce regressions on other instructions or scenes. A T3 variant trained without the earlier sources regressed on the reserve set and was rejected, so the final mixture kept them. Keeping an instruction’s demonstrations did not always preserve its performance: earlier T1 variants improved body grasping but lost cap success, and the selected T5 policy declined slightly on C. We therefore checked individual instructions when selecting checkpoints, even when the task score improved.

Repeated model selection and combined training changes limit what the local comparisons establish. The local sets served both to guide revisions and to select checkpoints, so their scores describe development performance and the reported intervals do not account for that selection. Official T1 and T5 scores were below their local values, while T2–T4 were within half a point; because the official and local suites use different scenes, these gaps do not identify a cause. Training rounds also combined demonstration and sampling changes, so the local comparisons do not isolate the effect of either.
